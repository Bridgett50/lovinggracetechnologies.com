Agents can pay now. The approval screen is the whole job.

Buying got easier. Deciding correctly did not.

Three news items landed within eight days, and they belong together. On Nov 5, 2026, Visa published a benchmark that put AI shopping agents through realistic buying tasks. On the same day, federal prosecutors in New York unsealed an indictment alleging that AI impersonation was used to trick employees into sending about $129 million. On Nov 13, 2026, OpenAI shipped ChatGPT Wallet, which lets an AI agent complete a purchase on a user's behalf. Buying got easier. Deciding correctly did not.

What Visa found

Visa's test is the clearest measure of the gap. The Visa Agentic Commerce Benchmark put 22 AI models from 12 labs through 15 shopping challenges, such as buying a TV, a laptop, or a personal care appliance. To pass, a model had to uncover the customer's requirements in conversation and then make the right choice, which sometimes meant not buying at all. The models passed 57.2 percent of runs and failed 42.8 percent. The best models passed 82.2 percent of attempts. The worst passed 28.9 percent. Consistency was weaker still. Each model got three attempts at each scenario, and all three attempts passed only 34.2 percent of the time.

The finding that matters most for anyone who lets an agent near money is this one. In 54 percent of the runs that failed, the AI still ended in a purchase. It bought something that did not meet the customer's stated requirements, or it bought before it had uncovered all the requirements. Failing did not mean stopping. Visa's own September 2026 Trust Index found that 72 percent of U.S. consumers had used an AI assistant, but only 23 percent trusted AI to handle payment transactions on their behalf. The benchmark explains why that trust is low, and what has to change to earn it.

What OpenAI shipped

OpenAI's Wallet, shipped Nov 13, 2026, is built around one answer to that problem: a human approval step, with the card itself kept out of the agent's reach. In OpenAI's release notes, the Wallet helps a user's dot go from finding what is needed to making a purchase on the user's behalf. The user saves an eligible card once, then reviews the merchant, the items, the total, and the selected card before authorizing with biometrics such as Face ID or Touch ID. The Wallet supplies a substitute payment credential at checkout, so the actual card details stay hidden from the agent and the business. The user keeps the card's rewards and its protections for qualifying disputes. The rollout is gradual, in dots, for ChatGPT Pro subscribers age 18 or older in the United States, with an eligible US-issued card and a US billing address. OpenAI adds a limit worth reading twice: approval alone does not confirm an order, and the merchant's confirmation still has to be checked after checkout.

What prosecutors allege

The indictment unsealed Nov 5, 2026, shows the other side of the same week. The Justice Department charged Jacob Soall, Sharon Hazan, and Guy Sheetrit with using what the release calls super intelligence to impersonate the voices and images of corporate executives and attorneys, in order to trick employees into sending large payments to accounts controlled by the alleged conspiracy. Prosecutors allege the scheme ran between 2024 and 2026 and took more than $129 million from companies in the United States and around the world, including six itemized losses ranging from about $2.7 million to about $11.6 million. The charges are allegations. The defendants are presumed innocent unless and until proven guilty, and extradition requests were pending at the time of the announcement. The practical lesson does not depend on the outcome of the case. A voice or a face on a call is no longer proof of identity. A payment request that arrives with urgency and secrecy still needs a check that runs outside the channel it arrived through, and a named person who signs off on it.

One job

Read together, the three items describe one job. The agent can find, compare, draft, and queue. The human must still decide, on a screen that shows exactly what will move, to whom, for how much, and under what terms, with a real option to say no. A tap that only confirms that something happened is not approval. Approval is a check that can stop the move.

Five checks worth keeping before any agent is allowed to pay

  1. The approval screen shows the merchant, the items, the quantity, the total including known taxes, fees, and shipping, the card being charged, and whether the purchase creates a recurring charge, a deposit, or an authorization hold. If any of that is missing, the screen is not ready.
  2. No purchase is allowed when the requirements are incomplete. The Visa result to remember is the 54 percent: a failed run that buys anyway is worse than a failed run that stops.
  3. A payment change, a new payee, or an urgent wire request is verified through a channel the request did not use. A callback to a known number, a separate message thread, or an in-person confirmation counts. A reply in the same thread does not.
  4. The agent never sees raw card details. A substitute credential, a token, or a wallet layer sits between the agent and the account, and the limits on that credential are set in advance.
  5. Every attempt is logged, including the ones that were stopped. When something goes wrong, the record should show what the agent proposed, what the approver saw, and what was decided.

What to watch

Whether later Visa benchmark rounds, which Visa says will extend to checkout, payment, and order placement, show the failure rate falling, and whether wallet-style approval screens become the default wherever agents can spend. The news this week says the tools to buy are here. The controls that make buying safe are still being built, one approval screen at a time.
