Congress wrote rules for federal AI agents.

On Nov 18, 2026, House Homeland Security Republicans introduced four bills for AI agents on federal networks: an investigations board, an agent framework, an AI hiring pilot, and a codified IT-sector role. They are bills, not law.

On Nov 18, 2026, House Homeland Security Republicans introduced four bills aimed at strengthening federal cybersecurity in the age of AI. Nextgov/FCW reported on the ROOT CAUSE Act and two related proposals, while the committee's own release also details the BRIDGE AI Act. Nextgov/FCW reported the bills on the morning of Nov 18, and the committee published its own release the same day.

The timing follows a bad week for shared systems. A nationwide FAA ground stop on Friday, Nov 13, delayed more than 10,000 flights. A separate Microsoft outage disrupted email and other workplace tools. An outage of NSANet, the classified network inside the National Security Agency, slowed analysts' work on Tuesday and forced schedule changes; NSA attributed that disruption to maintenance. Officials have not identified a cyberattack as the cause of the FAA or Microsoft events.

The first bill would create the investigator. The ROOT CAUSE Act, introduced by Rep. Morgan Luttrell of Texas with Rep. Ritchie Torres of New York as an original cosponsor, would establish a National Cyber and AI Investigations Board inside the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. The board would examine significant cyber incidents, including those involving AI or cyber terrorism, identify root causes, and recommend measures to prevent similar incidents. The committee's release adds the limits: the board would have no regulatory or enforcement authority, and its reviews would not assign civil or criminal liability. The bill also sets published incident selection criteria, a majority vote to open an investigation, and regular congressional oversight. Nextgov reports the bill would prevent the executive branch from abolishing, suspending, or consolidating the board without another act of Congress, and that officials outside the board could not alter or suppress its findings. National security systems like NSANet would fall outside the board's authority, though its reviews could cover other government and private-sector systems, including shared software and cloud services.

The proposal arrives nearly two years after the Trump administration emptied the Department of Homeland Security's Cyber Safety Review Board during its investigation of China's Salt Typhoon telecom intrusions. That board had previously faulted Microsoft for security failures that allowed Chinese hackers to access senior U.S. officials' emails.

The second bill would set the rules for the agents themselves. The AI INSIGHT Act, introduced by Rep. Matt Van Epps of Tennessee, would establish a framework for securing AI agents operating on federal civilian information systems, with CISA providing operational support, guidance, and technical assistance. The bill would direct CISA to help agencies identify the agents on their networks, track their actions, and limit their access to what a task requires. Agencies would be able to stop agents that exceed their authority and revoke access without waiting for a vendor's approval. The framework also calls for preserving the evidence behind AI-generated findings used in investigations or other official decisions. CISA would establish the supporting capabilities within a year of enactment. Defense and intelligence community systems, along with national security systems, would be excluded.

The other two bills fill in the workforce and the structure. The BRIDGE AI Act, introduced by Rep. Vince Fong of California, would establish a pilot program inside CISA to recruit AI and emerging technology experts from the private sector, academia, federally funded research and development centers, National Labs, and other technical talent pipelines. The SECURE IT Act, introduced by Rep. Andy Ogles of Tennessee, who chairs the Homeland Security cybersecurity subcommittee, would write the Department of Homeland Security's existing responsibility as the Sector Risk Management Agency for the Information Technology Sector into law, with DHS, through CISA, serving as the designated agency. Companies' participation would remain voluntary, and the bill calls for better coordination as AI speeds up the discovery and exploitation of software flaws, including testing fixes, deploying them in stages, and preserving the ability to reverse defective updates.

Nextgov also reports the Senate moved on the same theme the same day: Sens. Maria Cantwell of Washington and Mike Rounds of South Dakota wrote to Jay Clayton, the national intelligence director and chair of the White House Super Intelligence Task Force, urging him to coordinate a national response to AI-enabled cyber threats. They called for wider deployment of AI-powered defenses and security standards for autonomous agents. They cited AI's role in uncovering flaws in PHP, software used to run websites, and reports that banks could not always distinguish authorized AI agents accessing customer accounts from rogue actors.

The committee's own background note adds one more thread: in May, Anthropic gave committee members one of the first live demonstrations of Mythos given to Congress, showing how an advanced model can find software vulnerabilities. The demonstration reinforced, in the committee's words, the urgency of ensuring federal agencies can responsibly use advanced U.S. models to find and patch vulnerabilities.

These are bills, not law. They arrive before Democrats take control of Congress in January, Nextgov reports, with Rep. Bennie Thompson of Mississippi positioned to chair the House homeland panel. Whether the board, the agent framework, or the hiring pilot survives that handoff is the open question.

Five things to watch

  1. Whether the ROOT CAUSE Act keeps its independence protections: no abolition without an act of Congress, and no outside alteration of findings.
  2. Whether the AI INSIGHT Act's one-year deadline for CISA's supporting capabilities holds, and what those capabilities actually cover.
  3. Whether agencies get real revocation power over agents that exceed their authority, without a vendor's approval.
  4. Whether the evidence-preservation rule for AI-generated findings changes how agencies document decisions.
  5. Whether the Senate letter turns into its own bill, or stays a request for coordination.
