The agent control plane grew up: from permission to proof of value.

Appian shared new agent control plane capabilities at Appian Europe in London on Nov 17, 2026. The control plane registers agents, runs them inside deterministic business processes, and measures them on the KPIs of the process they touch. A 2026 study by Appian and Harvard Business Review Analytic Services found just 16 percent of respondents report a high degree of measurable value from AI.

On Nov 17, 2026, at Appian Europe in London, Appian shared new agent management capabilities for its platform. The control plane covers three areas: track which agents are running and keep them in bounds, orchestrate agents and other digital workers to get work done, and optimize agents for better business outcomes. Many of the capabilities are available today, including controls, orchestration, and process intelligence, with additional capabilities starting this year. Customers can join an early adopter program to use the control plane ahead of general availability.

The reason for the announcement is a number. A 2026 study conducted by Appian and Harvard Business Review Analytic Services found that just 16 percent of respondents reported a high degree of measurable value from AI. Appian's executive vice president of product management, Sanat Joshi, said concerns about agent safety are driving demand for control planes to enforce permissions, guardrails, and model access, and added: "Those matter, but they can't tell you whether an agent is doing useful work." The control plane question is shifting. Permission was the first question. Value is the next one.

The three areas answer it in order. Track: teams register Appian and third-party agents in one place, monitor their activity and cost, control which models agents can use, track policy compliance, and set alerts. Orchestrate: agents work inside deterministic business processes, where each step is assigned to the worker best suited to the task, an agent, a person, a system, or a rule. If an agent fails or is stopped, the process routes its task to a person to keep work moving. Optimize: each agent is measured on the key performance indicators of the business process it impacts, evaluations track agent accuracy, and insights show which repeat agent steps can be converted to deterministic workflows and business rules for better cost, speed, and accuracy.

The example Appian gave is a bank. Raiffeisen Bank International intends to automate payment inquiries with the Appian platform. In testing, the team found that orchestrating and fine-tuning AI agents alongside rules-based automation significantly reduced processing time and token consumption. Claus Mitterlehner, head of smart automation at the bank, said the team's first instinct was to assign most tasks to AI agents, then changed course after testing and moved the predictable steps to deterministic automation, keeping agents for the steps that need judgment. The result, in the bank's own words, is now more economical and more reliable, and the hybrid approach reduced token consumption and processing time by 80 percent in testing. Appian's own page for the study, What Drives AI Value: Why Modernization and Workflow Integration Matter, puts the gap plainly: 92 percent of respondents said agents need rules-based guardrails to be safe and effective, while 48 percent actually had such rules in place.

Appian also unveiled Ask Appian, a new agentic interface. Users describe the outcome they want in plain language, and Ask Appian pulls the relevant context across systems connected through Appian's data fabric and dispatches agents to complete the work end to end, without the employee navigating complex enterprise software or hunting across disconnected systems for information.

Five checks worth keeping before an agent control plane earns its keep

  1. Measure the agent on the process KPI, not on activity. An agent that ran a thousand times and changed nothing is not productive. Measure the agent against the key performance indicators of the business process it impacts. Appian says its control plane evaluates agent accuracy and identifies repeat steps that could become deterministic workflows or business rules.
  2. Put agents inside a deterministic process with a person fallback. If an agent fails or is stopped, the work should route to a person, not stall or guess.
  3. Use rules for the predictable and agents for judgment. Raiffeisen's test is the evidence: the predictable steps moved to deterministic automation, and the agents kept the steps that need judgment.
  4. Register every agent in one place, including third-party agents. A consolidated register gives teams a central point for monitoring agent activity and cost, controlling model access, tracking policy compliance, and setting alerts.
  5. Base buying decisions only on features generally available today. Appian's own release says planned features and release timing are subject to change and are not a commitment. Customers can join Appian's early-adopter program to access control-plane capabilities ahead of general availability.

What to watch

whether other agent platforms add value measurement to their control planes, whether the 16 percent figure moves as control planes spread, and whether plain-language interfaces like Ask Appian become the normal way people ask enterprise systems for work. The news this week is that the control plane is no longer only about permission. It is about proof: what the agent did, what it cost, and whether the business is better for it.
