Devin Bots turn coding agents into teams managed around outcomes

Cognition launched Devin Bots on Nov 17, 2026. A Bot takes a goal, leads a team of Devin coding agents, keeps the memory, and brings only the decisions that need a human back to the engineer.

On Nov 17, 2026, Cognition launched Devin Bots. A Bot is a persistent agent with its own memory and tools that can lead multiple Devin coding agents in parallel. You give it a large project or an ongoing goal. It investigates, delegates work, and evaluates the results to decide what to pursue next. It retains context across sessions and brings you only the decisions that need your judgment. The shift is in the assignment. Until now, working with a coding agent meant delegating specific tasks. A Bot takes on a responsibility instead. "Port this feature to Android" is a task. "Keep Android at parity with iOS" is a responsibility, and the Bot decides what to build next to meet it.

The Android test: 44 days, 1,134 pull requests

Cognition ran the test on itself first. Devin had an iOS app and a team of iOS engineers, and no Android engineers and no plan for Android. On Sep 28, 2026, Samyok, an engineer on Cognition's Bots team, created Android Bot and initially instructed it to product manage a new Android app, identify the blockers, and work toward building the app. Samyok established the direction and constraints, while the Bot managed the resulting work. Forty-four days later, Devin had an Android app. The Bot surfaced blockers first, such as where the code would live and how to test an Android build, then filed tickets, started a Devin session for each one, and merged the pull requests that passed CI and Devin Review. Each day it posted a report in Slack with a screen recording of what had changed. The totals from the announcement: 1,134 pull requests merged, 344,651 lines of Kotlin, 2,841 screenshot tests, 1,217 tickets closed. The engineer sent the Bot 183 messages over those 44 days, about one for every six pull requests merged, and on 22 of the days he sent none. Most of his messages addressed something that needed a human decision, such as a pull request that needed a security review. The app is now in open testing on Google Play, and the Android Bot owns its development end to end, updating it every time a new feature lands on iOS. These are company-reported figures from Cognition's own test, not independent results.

The layout watch and the game studio

Two more responsibilities show what a Bot does when nobody is watching. The Layout Bot owns keeping the Devin web app from shifting or lagging as it changes. Every three hours it builds the production version of the latest code, runs the full stack on its own machine, and simulates production loading conditions. When a check fails, it reruns it to rule out timing noise, traces the regression to the pull request that introduced it, and opens a fix with before-and-after recordings for an engineer to review. Cognition reports that during the Layout Bot's first three weeks of operation, the company merged 159 of its pull requests, fixing layout shifts and reducing lag by 70 percent. The announcement does not provide the detailed methodology behind that percentage. Cognition says it merges more than 2,000 pull requests a week across its own teams.

The third test is a mobile game studio run by one person. Jenny Xu is the studio's only human employee, with about 20 Bots, each owning one job end to end: development, Unity, releases, UI, iOS, Android, testing on real devices. Above them is a Super Jenny Bot, the only one she talks to. Four times a day it collects the decisions the other Bots need from her into one Slack thread, and she answers with emoji. According to Cognition, Jenny Xu's studio increased its weekly pull-request volume from approximately 50 to 1,900 and now ships a release each week. The figures describe development activity and release frequency, not independently measured improvements in product quality or revenue. Since the studio started running on Devin Bots, she joined Cognition full time.

What the human still does

Cognition describes an operating model in which engineers remain responsible for defining success, establishing access and approval boundaries, and determining how Bots evaluate their own results. The Android and Layout case studies illustrate extensive delegation, but do not establish that human oversight can be eliminated. Engineers define what success means for the scope they give a Bot, including which checks must remain and whether spending more on compute is acceptable. They define how independently a Bot can act: which systems it can access, what it can change, and when spending or releases require approval. They build the feedback the Bot evaluates itself against: tests, benchmarks, user flows in the lab, and production behavior and customer feedback in the wild. Bots share reports and test recordings proactively, and decisions that need input land in a Needs You inbox. The engineer can open individual Devin sessions to dig into a workstream, share Bots with the team, and meet them on Slack, iMessage, or in the Devin app. The human's job moves from specifying work to deciding what is worth owning, setting the bar for it, and building the systems that track whether it is met.

Availability and the fine print

The announcement says Devin Bots are available today at devin.ai/bots. No price is stated in the announcement. The customer quotes in the post, from Fireworks AI, Reducto, Decagon, Bilt, and OpenRouter, are the company's own selected quotes, not independent verification. Coverage of the launch describes early access with dozens of customers and usage growing tenfold in two weeks, reported by Fortune; that is secondary reporting, not verified on a primary source, and it sits next to the announcement's own "available today" wording, so the exact breadth of access is not settled from the sources read. What is settled is the direction: the coding-agent race has moved from who writes code fastest to who can run a whole project while the human is away, and Cognition shipped the manager first in this round. Anthropic's Duet and OpenAI's Dots, rival always-on agents shipped in the preceding weeks, are named in the coverage as the competition.

What to watch

Whether manager agents hold up outside Cognition's own teams, where the Bots run on the company's own product and its own engineers. Whether the Needs You inbox stays manageable as the number of Bots grows, or becomes the new backlog. Whether a Bot finding work nobody assigned, which the announcement presents as a strength, stays useful or starts generating work nobody asked for. And whether the human's remaining job, deciding what is worth owning and what counts as done, turns out to be the real skill the next generation of engineers has to learn.
