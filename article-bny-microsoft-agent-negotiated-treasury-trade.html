Research and Analysis

Two agents negotiated a Treasury trade. Humans still approved it.

On Nov 17, 2026, BNY announced that it and Microsoft had completed a pilot in which AI agents from the two firms negotiated a US Treasury bill trade under defined controls, while human traders reviewed and approved the proposed terms before execution.

BNY announced on Nov 17, 2026 what it calls an industry-first pilot trade negotiation of a US Treasury bill using AI agents. The agents exchanged offers and counteroffers and settled on terms inside preset limits. They did not execute anything. Human traders at both firms reviewed and approved the proposed terms before the trade was completed, and the booking went through the firms' existing systems. The negotiation was the news. The approval was the point.

The control layer is the announcement

At the center of the trade was BNY's Agentic Negotiation Protocol, or ANP, a patent-pending control layer for authorized agents from different organizations to negotiate with each other. ANP verifies the participants and lets them connect securely, then lets the agents exchange offers and counteroffers and confirms both sides share the same understanding of the proposed terms. Participant-side controls set predefined rules and AI safety checks, keeping the agents within tightly defined parameters based on approved pricing inputs. Before trader approval is requested, ANP's ledger checks that both parties' agents submitted matching terms. The process preserves a record of who participated, the messages exchanged, the control checks, and the human decisions, so the negotiation can be reviewed and reconstructed later.

Three limits are stated plainly in the release. Agents cannot execute the trade on behalf of either firm. Human traders retain authority over final decisions and actions. Trades are booked through existing internal trading systems, not through the agent layer. The agents negotiated. People decided. Existing systems recorded it.

How the trade happened

Microsoft Treasury gave its agent specific trading instructions, and the agent connected to BNY through a secure agent gateway to ANP. BNY controls validated the counterparty, the security, the size, the settlement terms, and the available pricing. Once those checks passed, the agents negotiated prices within their authorized limits. When they reached agreement, the proposed terms were independently submitted for comparison against ANP's central ledger to confirm both parties had the same understanding of the transaction. BNY and Microsoft traders then reviewed and approved the proposed terms, with execution and booking completed through the firms' existing systems and controls. Bloomberg reported the same day that the agents haggled and agreed on a price before humans approved the transaction.

What the release does not say

The release states no trade size, no maturity, no yield, and no price. It does not say how long the negotiation took, or how many offers and counteroffers passed between the agents. The announcement describes one pilot trade negotiation between BNY and Microsoft, and "industry-first" is BNY's characterization, not an independently established global first. The release also does not claim the agents trade better than humans. It claims the process is more consistent than the traditional message-based negotiation, and that it frees traders to focus on higher-value work while keeping human accountability and existing risk and transaction-processing controls in place. Those are claims about the process, not about the price.

Where it goes next

BNY plans to continue testing ANP, with a focus on expanding beyond the initial bilateral use case and evaluating additional negotiated workflows. Over time, future applications could include repo and other contractual negotiations. The pattern to watch is governed autonomy, not full automation: authorized agents, defined limits, a matching-terms check, human approval, and a complete record. That is the same shape as every approval-gated system in this series, now tested on a Treasury trade at a bank that oversees $63.4 trillion in assets under custody and administration and $2.2 trillion in assets under management, as of Sep 30, 2026.

What to watch

Whether other banks and dealers join the protocol, and whether it holds up outside a bilateral pilot with a willing counterparty. Whether the ledger check catches a mismatch in a live test, and what happens when it does. Whether regulators ask to see the record ANP preserves, and whether that record satisfies them. And whether the next test, repo or a contractual negotiation, keeps the same rule: the agents can bargain, but only people can commit.
