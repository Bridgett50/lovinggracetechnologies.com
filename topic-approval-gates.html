The agent control plane grew up: from permission to proof of value.

Appian shared new agent control plane capabilities at Appian Europe in London on Nov 17, 2026. The control plane registers agents, runs them inside deterministic business processes, and measures them on the KPIs of the process they touch. A 2026 study by Appian and Harvard Business Review Analytic Services found just 16 percent of respondents report a high degree of measurable value from AI.

On Nov 17, 2026, at Appian Europe in London, Appian shared new agent management capabilities for its platform. The control plane covers three areas: track which agents are running and keep them in bounds, orchestrate agents and other digital workers to get work done, and optimize agents for better business outcomes. Many of the capabilities are available today, including controls, orchestration, and process intelligence, with additional capabilities starting this year. Customers can join an early adopter program to use the control plane ahead of general availability.

The reason for the announcement is a number. A 2026 study conducted by Appian and Harvard Business Review Analytic Services found that just 16 percent of respondents reported a high degree of measurable value from AI. Appian's executive vice president of product management, Sanat Joshi, said concerns about agent safety are driving demand for control planes to enforce permissions, guardrails, and model access, and added: "Those matter, but they can't tell you whether an agent is doing useful work." The control plane question is shifting. Permission was the first question. Value is the next one.

The three areas answer it in order. Track: teams register Appian and third-party agents in one place, monitor their activity and cost, control which models agents can use, track policy compliance, and set alerts. Orchestrate: agents work inside deterministic business processes, where each step is assigned to the worker best suited to the task, an agent, a person, a system, or a rule. If an agent fails or is stopped, the process routes its task to a person to keep work moving. Optimize: each agent is measured on the key performance indicators of the business process it impacts, evaluations track agent accuracy, and insights show which repeat agent steps can be converted to deterministic workflows and business rules for better cost, speed, and accuracy.

The example Appian gave is a bank. Raiffeisen Bank International intends to automate payment inquiries with the Appian platform. In testing, the team found that orchestrating and fine-tuning AI agents alongside rules-based automation significantly reduced processing time and token consumption. Claus Mitterlehner, head of smart automation at the bank, said the team's first instinct was to assign most tasks to AI agents, then changed course after testing and moved the predictable steps to deterministic automation, keeping agents for the steps that need judgment. The result, in the bank's own words, is now more economical and more reliable, and the hybrid approach reduced token consumption and processing time by 80 percent in testing. Appian's own page for the study, What Drives AI Value: Why Modernization and Workflow Integration Matter, puts the gap plainly: 92 percent of respondents said agents need rules-based guardrails to be safe and effective, while 48 percent actually had such rules in place.

Appian also unveiled Ask Appian, a new agentic interface. Users describe the outcome they want in plain language, and Ask Appian pulls the relevant context across systems connected through Appian's data fabric and dispatches agents to complete the work end to end, without the employee navigating complex enterprise software or hunting across disconnected systems for information.

Five checks worth keeping before an agent control plane earns its keep

  1. Measure the agent on the process KPI, not on activity. An agent that ran a thousand times and changed nothing is not productive. Measure the agent against the key performance indicators of the business process it impacts. Appian says its control plane evaluates agent accuracy and identifies repeat steps that could become deterministic workflows or business rules.
  2. Put agents inside a deterministic process with a person fallback. If an agent fails or is stopped, the work should route to a person, not stall or guess.
  3. Use rules for the predictable and agents for judgment. Raiffeisen's test is the evidence: the predictable steps moved to deterministic automation, and the agents kept the steps that need judgment.
  4. Register every agent in one place, including third-party agents. A consolidated register gives teams a central point for monitoring agent activity and cost, controlling model access, tracking policy compliance, and setting alerts.
  5. Base buying decisions only on features generally available today. Appian's own release says planned features and release timing are subject to change and are not a commitment. Customers can join Appian's early-adopter program to access control-plane capabilities ahead of general availability.

What to watch

whether other agent platforms add value measurement to their control planes, whether the 16 percent figure moves as control planes spread, and whether plain-language interfaces like Ask Appian become the normal way people ask enterprise systems for work. The news this week is that the control plane is no longer only about permission. It is about proof: what the agent did, what it cost, and whether the business is better for it.

Earned autonomy: the agent proposes, the expert approves, and the leash gets longer.

Genpact announced Genpact Onyx on Nov 16, 2026. Agents start by proposing actions for a human expert to approve and take on more only as they prove themselves, with every action controlled, auditable, and traceable to the reason behind the decision.

On Nov 16, 2026, Genpact announced the availability of Genpact Onyx, a platform for building, running, and governing AI agents in enterprise operations. It was unveiled four days earlier, on Nov 12, at Genpact's Agentic Operations Day in New York. The platform's idea is easy to state and hard to build: agents should start with context on how the work actually gets done, and earn more freedom only by proving themselves, one approved decision at a time.

The context comes from a layer Genpact calls the Process Intelligence Core. It is operating knowledge codified from decades of running mission-critical work for large enterprises, across finance, risk, supply chain, banking, insurance, and procurement, with prebuilt workflows and agents so clients are ready on day one. The release makes a point worth keeping: that knowledge comes from doing the actual work and capturing the edge cases, exceptions, and human decisions that rarely show up in a prompt library or a static process map. An agent that starts with that context does not have to learn the job from scratch, and does not have to guess at the exceptions.

The approval step is the mechanism. Genpact calls it earned autonomy. Agents start by proposing actions for a human expert to approve and take on more of the decision as they prove themselves. Reviewed exceptions and expert decisions feed back into the Process Intelligence Core, so the intelligence compounds over time and the next case moves faster and more autonomously. The leash gets longer because the record got better, not because someone decided to trust the agent more.

Governance is built in rather than added on. Every agent action in Genpact Onyx is controlled, auditable, and traceable to the reason behind the decision, with guardrails and a responsible AI framework built into the platform, and security and data isolation that meet enterprise standards. The platform embeds into a client's existing technology stack, connects to the client's data and AI foundations, and supports a choice of leading AI models across Genpact's partner ecosystem.

The first product built on the platform is the Genpact Accounts Payable Suite. Genpact presents projected estimates for the suite of up to 80 percent touchless processing, more than 95 percent duplicate prevention, and up to 90 percent of supplier queries resolved automatically. Those are the company's own projected estimates, reported by Unite.AI, not measured results from a live deployment, and they should be read that way. Genpact's chief executive, Balkrishan Kalra, put the operating model in one line: "Agents run the work. Our experts own the outcome." Priya Vijayarajendran, the chief product and platform officer, said the platform "lets autonomy be earned one decision at a time."

Five checks worth keeping before an agent earns more freedom

  1. Start with proposals, not actions. An agent that proposes for approval can be wrong safely. An agent that acts first can only be corrected after the fact.
  2. Ground the agent in how the work is actually done, including the exceptions. A prompt library and a process map are not the same as the decisions people made when the map did not cover the case.
  3. Keep every action traceable to its reason. If the record cannot show why the agent did what it did, the approval that follows it is guesswork.
  4. Read projected figures as projections. Up to 80 percent touchless processing is a company estimate for a new suite, not a measured result. Ask what has been measured, and where.
  5. Ask what happens when the agent is unsure. Earned autonomy only works if an unsure agent proposes and waits, instead of acting and hoping. The approval step has to be the default, not the exception.

What to watch

whether other agent platforms adopt earned autonomy as the model, with approval first and freedom granted on the record; whether the Accounts Payable Suite's projected figures become measured results from live clients; and whether process knowledge, the record of how work actually gets done, becomes the deciding factor when enterprises choose a platform. The news this week is that the leash is no longer a fixed length. It grows, one approved decision at a time.

Microsoft's Ignite answer: govern the agent, measure it, and give it an identity.

At Microsoft Ignite 2026 on Nov 17, Microsoft framed Copilot as the new operating system for work, put Autopilot in Frontier rollout as a long-running agent with its own identity and memory, and added Agent 365 cost management and business analytics. The governance arrived at the same time as the agents, and most of the new pieces are still in preview or the Frontier program.

Microsoft Ignite 2026 opened in San Francisco on Nov 17, 2026, and runs through Nov 20. Chairman and CEO Satya Nadella opened with a keynote framed around what Microsoft calls a frontier ecosystem: people and agents working together, organizations keeping control of their own intelligence, and trust in the technology. His three pillars were human ambition, intelligence, and trust. The announcements below are the ones that change how an organization runs agents, and the availability label on each one matters as much as the feature.

Microsoft describes Copilot as the new operating system for work, positioning it as a central place for conversations, delegated tasks, document creation, and collaboration. The new Copilot brings together Home, which is rolling out worldwide as the starting point for Chat and Cowork; Auto, which can recommend moving complex tasks into Cowork and includes additional model-routing capabilities for Frontier customers; and Office in Copilot, now generally available, which brings Word, Excel, and PowerPoint into conversations for document creation and collaboration. Files are saved to OneDrive and inherit Microsoft 365 permissions, sensitivity labels, and compliance controls. Copilot Code lets users build dashboards, trackers, and apps using natural language. Cloud projects run in a secure sandbox within the organization's tenant, while local sandbox protections are available on Windows and planned for macOS. Copilot Code is available through the Frontier program on Windows, web, iOS, and Android, with general availability planned for December 2026. Copilot Workspaces gives teams, their work, and Copilot a shared space in the Copilot app, and it is available through the Frontier program. Copilot Managed Runtime, the Microsoft-managed hosting and execution layer for what people build in Copilot, is now generally available, with an optional Fabric data backend.

Autopilot is the announcement to read twice. Microsoft calls it its first enterprise-grade, long-running agent for personal productivity. It lives in an organization's tenant with its own identity, memory, and access to tools. It can follow up on threads, run recurring work, and continue a project without waiting for another prompt. Colleagues will be able to @mention a person's Autopilot in a Teams channel or group chat to ask for information or request supported actions. The security design is stated in the Book of News: actions Autopilot takes, even on a user's behalf, are tied to its own agent ID and safety-checked, and Autopilot is visible and manageable by admins. Autopilot is rolling out through the Frontier program, Microsoft's early-access route, and it will appear in the left navigation of the Copilot app. Teams will soon be able to bring their personal Autopilot into Workspaces, so humans and agents collaborate in one space.

The intelligence layer is Microsoft IQ and Foundry. Microsoft IQ is the enterprise context management platform that helps organizations build their own IQ by capturing, governing, and reusing the business knowledge that apps and agents need. New business plugins let teams package that knowledge into reusable, trusted context, skills, and tools shared across the business. In Microsoft Foundry, new continuous improvement tools help customers develop more powerful agents and train models automatically based on real production signals, optimizing for quality, latency, and cost. Microsoft-Decision-1.1, a fast, low-cost decision model for routing, classification, prioritization, verification, and workflow control, is now generally available. Microsoft is also connecting Copilot to the systems teams already use: Salesforce in Copilot is in preview with more than 38 pre-built sales skills, and Dynamics 365 Sales and ERP plugins are now generally available.

Microsoft announced its trust and governance capabilities alongside its agents, with availability ranging from generally available security products to Frontier releases, previews, and planned enhancements. Microsoft Perception, now generally available, brings together specialized agents to find, defend, and remediate vulnerabilities, powered by models including Microsoft's MAI-Cyber line. Agent 365 Business Analytics, now available in the Frontier program, gives business leaders data on AI spend, task success, user satisfaction, and assisted hours, so they can see what AI costs and what it delivers. Insights for Microsoft services require at least one Microsoft 365 Copilot license in the tenant; insights for non-Microsoft services require an Agent 365 license. Agent 365 Cost Management, in preview, is a centralized place to see and control usage-based AI services, set spending policies, limits, and alerts, and extend analysis through Microsoft Fabric for chargeback and reporting. Microsoft says spending forecasts and optimization recommendations are available through the Frontier program in November 2026, while an expansion covering value insights across Microsoft and non-Microsoft agents and delegation to business leaders is planned for December. Windows is expanding operating-system-level protection for agents, with Microsoft Execution Containers, built-in agent identity with Entra support, and Agent 365 policies enforced on every device. BNY, building on Microsoft Defender, Entra, and Purview, is exploring capabilities in Project Perception, and, according to Microsoft, BNY's collaboration completed the first agentic negotiation of a US Treasury trade.

The infrastructure underneath is Azure Cobalt 200 virtual machines, now generally available, delivering up to 50 percent better performance than the previous generation, according to Microsoft. One discrepancy to note: Microsoft's global blog says Cobalt 200 will reach 23 Azure regions by year end, while the Book of News says 24 regions by the end of the year. The performance claim matches on both pages; the region count does not, and this draft uses neither number as settled. Azure Catalyst, a unified experience for agentic modernization across Azure and GitHub, is generally available. Customer scale in the keynote: Volkswagen Group is equipping more than 330,000 employees with Copilot, and The Estée Lauder Companies has expanded Copilot to more than 14,000 people. Publicis Groupe is empowering employees to build custom AI skills that combine Microsoft IQ with its industry expertise and proprietary data.

Five checks worth keeping before an organization adopts the Ignite announcements

  1. Check the availability label before planning a rollout. Autopilot, Workspaces, and Copilot Code are in the Frontier program. Agent 365 Cost Management is in preview. Copilot Managed Runtime, Office in Copilot, Microsoft Perception, Cobalt 200, and Azure Catalyst are generally available. A plan built on a Frontier feature is a plan built on early access.
  2. Give the agent an identity you can audit. Autopilot acts under its own agent ID, and its actions are safety-checked and visible to admins. Ask for the log of what it did, who it did it for, and what it touched.
  3. Price the agent before it runs. Agent 365 Cost Management sets spending policies, limits, and alerts across usage-based AI services. Set them before the services pile up, not after the first bill.
  4. Decide who owns the context. Microsoft IQ packages company knowledge into reusable context, skills, and tools. Decide who approves what goes into that package, and who can change it.
  5. Keep a person in the approval path for money and commitments. BNY's agentic Treasury trade shows agents can negotiate. Decide in advance which actions need a person, and write it down before the agent is given the job.

What to watch

whether Copilot Code reaches general availability in December as planned; whether Autopilot widens beyond the Frontier program; whether Agent 365 Cost Management's December expansion to non-Microsoft agents ships; what Agent 365 licensing costs once the analytics move past Frontier; and whether Microsoft corrects the Cobalt 200 region count. The pattern at Ignite was consistent: the agent got a job, an identity, a budget, and a log, in the same announcement. That is the standard to hold every agent platform to.

Twilio grades your AI agent after the call ends.

Twilio added two supervision operators on Nov 17, 2026. Task Completion says whether the first task in a conversation was resolved. Hallucination Detection flags claims that conflict with the knowledge base or the conversation itself.

On Nov 17, 2026, Twilio added AI agent supervision to Twilio Conversations. The announcement adds two operators to Conversation Intelligence, Twilio's product for analyzing conversations. Task Completion shows whether the agent resolved the customer's request. Hallucination Detection flags when the agent said something that conflicts with the knowledge base it was given or with the conversation itself. Both run when a conversation ends. Conversation Insights, an API, then aggregates the results across conversations so teams can see patterns in what keeps going wrong.

The shift is in where the grading happens. Many AI observability tools look inside the agent, at traces, tool calls, and latency. Twilio starts from the conversation instead. If the conversations already run on Twilio, Twilio holds the customer-facing record of what happened, and the operators review that record whether the agent runs on one model, several models, or an external agent platform. Twilio says customers do not need to modify the agent's code to use conversation-based supervision. However, they must configure Conversation Orchestrator and Conversation Intelligence, attach the operators to an intelligence configuration, and supply the appropriate Enterprise Knowledge resources.

The two signals answer different questions, and Twilio says neither is enough on its own. Task Completion evaluates the first task in a conversation and assigns one of four labels: resolved, unresolved, escalated, or declined by the agent. It provides a one-sentence reason identifying the moment behind the classification. It does not separately grade every task the customer may raise. Hallucination Detection checks claims the agent makes against the knowledge articles shared with it and against the rest of the conversation, and returns the conflicting claims, the flagged utterance, and what the source says. A task can be completed and a conversation can end politely while the agent still gives the customer inaccurate information. Outcome and accuracy have to be checked together.

The judge is a model, and it is not the agent's model. Each operator uses a large language model as a judge. It reads the conversation, applies the operator's instructions, and returns a structured result. The judge is separate from the model behind the agent, so the agent is not grading its own work. Twilio says plainly that like any model, a judge can be wrong, so every result comes with a reason or the flagged utterance to check. The results are for finding conversations worth reviewing, not as a final verdict.

The timing and the limits matter. Results are available approximately 15 minutes after a conversation ends and can be analyzed through Conversation Insights. Individual operator results can also be sent to a customer-configured webhook. They are not real-time, so this supervision cannot stop a bad answer while the customer is still on the line. Twilio makes the operators available to customers as a public beta, subject to the required Conversations configuration. The products are available through the new Twilio Console, and accounts that have not migrated to that Console cannot access them there. Twilio's own documentation states that Task Completion and Hallucination Detection are not PCI compliant or HIPAA Eligible Services and should not be used in workflows subject to PCI or HIPAA requirements. Twilio also states that these public beta products are not covered by its support terms or service-level agreement. The conversations have to run on Twilio, because Twilio's record of the conversation is what the operators analyze.

On November 17, Twilio announced additional voice AI capabilities across Conversation Relay and Agent Connect. The ElevenLabs v4 Turbo conversational voice integration was described as generally available, while Smart Turn Detection, HD Audio, dynamic configuration parameters, and speech-to-text failover were announced for general availability during November 2026. The two announcements belong together: one makes voice agents sound more natural and stay connected, and the other grades what they said after the call.

Five checks worth keeping before an agent supervision tool earns its keep

  1. Grade on the conversation, not the trace. Traces show what the agent did. The conversation record shows what the customer experienced. Both matter, but only the conversation says whether the customer got the right answer.
  2. Pair outcome with accuracy. A resolved label and a correct answer are different facts. Check both, every time, or a polite wrong answer will pass as a success. Remember the first-task limit: a resolved label covers the first task only.
  3. Treat the judge's verdict as a review queue, not a verdict. The judge is a separate model, and it can be wrong. Every result comes with a reason or a flagged utterance. Read it before acting on it.
  4. Know what the supervision cannot see. It reviews conversations that run on Twilio, and the results arrive about 15 minutes after the conversation ends. It cannot grade calls on another platform, and it cannot stop a bad answer in real time. Task Completion grades only the first task in a conversation, so a call that raises a second problem is not fully graded.
  5. Keep the knowledge base current. Hallucination Detection checks the agent's claims against the knowledge articles it was given. If those articles are out of date, the judge grades against the wrong source, and the right answer can look like a hallucination.

What to watch

whether other agent platforms add conversation-record supervision of their own, whether the 15-minute delay shortens toward real time, and whether hallucination detection becomes a standard check wherever agents talk to customers, or stays a feature of one platform. The news this week is that supervision moved from inside the agent to the conversation itself, and the grade arrives after the call, with the evidence attached.

One governed door for every agent.

At SC26, Dell put a customer-controlled gateway between production AI agents and enterprise data. One front door, one identity per call, credentials held centrally, and the gateway is designed to operate inside the customer's environment without retaining prompts or persistent secrets, though some integrated services may still transmit selected context to external models under applicable customer agreements.

Dell is addressing a growing infrastructure challenge: giving independently developed AI agents consistent access to enterprise information without creating separate credentials, data connections, and oversight systems for each one. Its November 16 announcement introduces a centralized gateway designed to bring those functions together. When each agent builds its own connection to search, databases, and pipelines, and holds its own API keys, teams end up with scattered credentials, repeated retrieval work, and no single record of what any agent saw or did. Dell's answer is a single governed front door: the Agentic Data Gateway, part of the Dell AI Data Platform.

The gateway is a customer-controlled Model Context Protocol server and AI agent gateway. It gives every agent one front door to the platform's models, tools, knowledge, and data engines. Applications can communicate with the gateway through MCP or OpenAI-compatible interfaces. The system maintains a directory of available enterprise services and can direct requests to supported models, databases, search tools, and outside MCP integrations. Outside services can be registered too, so agents can reach record systems, alerting tools, and web search through the same door.

Three controls in the design are the ones that matter for anyone running agents against real data. First, each call runs with the caller's identity, data scope, and budget, and is traced end to end. Second, credentials are managed centrally, so agents no longer need to carry their own API keys. Retrieval, authorization, quotas, token accounting, and observability are handled in one place, giving teams an auditable path to thousands of concurrent agents. Dell says the gateway is designed to operate inside the customer's environment without retaining prompts or persistent secrets. Some integrated services may still transmit selected context to external models under applicable customer agreements, so local gateway operation does not mean every associated AI workload remains entirely on-premises. Dell's framing is that context is built once from governed data and then reused, so agents stop rebuilding the same understanding on every request.

Dell reports that context reuse reduced token consumption by as much as 76% and improved answer accuracy from 60% to 94% in its September 2026 internal benchmark. The test used a synthetic toll-plaza dataset, 20 questions, six workload types, and an NVIDIA Nemotron model. Dell cautions that results depend on the workload, data, model, and configuration; the figures are not evidence of equivalent performance across enterprise deployments. As an example of its broader AI Data Platform, Dell cites CTBC Bank, reporting fraud-detection times reduced from weeks to 0.03 seconds and more than $60 million in prevented fraud losses during 2025. Dell does not establish in this announcement that the newly introduced Agentic Data Gateway independently caused those outcomes.

The gateway sits inside a wider set of Data Orchestration additions. Hybrid orchestration lets pipelines run where the data and compute already live, on Kubernetes clusters, Dell AI Factory infrastructure, non-Dell infrastructure, and public cloud endpoints, with Dell deploying, scaling, observing, and tearing down the containers. Storage Intelligence scans storage arrays to build a metadata repository of the files they hold, so teams can see what they have and curate the relevant files into datasets. Dell's proposed Data Intelligence tools would help operators investigate AI outputs by connecting the underlying datasets with information about their origin, processing history, and quality. The announcement does not establish how completely these tools reconstruct every action across an agent workflow. A new structured-data browser brings tables into the same pipelines as unstructured content such as images, video, PDFs, and document chunks.

Dell announced integrations involving LangChain, OpenAI Codex, and Unstructured.io. The planned LangSmith integration will provide tracing and evaluation through Dell's platform marketplace. Codex development environments are designed to keep code execution and enterprise data processing on customer-controlled infrastructure, while sending necessary inference context to OpenAI-hosted models subject to customer agreements. Unstructured.io supplies document parsing, chunking, and embedding, with ready-to-use templates that turn raw documents into searchable knowledge and a chat tool that answers with sources cited.

Dell expects availability of its AI Data Platform improvements to begin in December 2026, without specifying a separate launch date for each announced gateway component or integration. The new Exascale storage platforms are scheduled for the first half of 2027.

What to watch

the release schedule for individual gateway capabilities, independently reported production performance, the security implications of combining internal and external AI services, and whether customers achieve measurable reductions in duplicated integrations and token usage.

Microsoft gave its agents their own accounts. The memory is shared.

On Nov 18, 2026, Microsoft said dedicated agent accounts for Copilot Studio agents will enter public preview in December 2026. An agent gets its own credentials, its own mailbox, and a place in the directory. The memory it builds is shared across everyone who works with it, with no opt-out in the preview.

On Nov 18, 2026, Microsoft said dedicated agent accounts for Copilot Studio agents will enter public preview in December 2026. An agent gets managed credentials and access to approved resources, so it can work in Microsoft 365 the way a person would. People can invoke it in Teams and Outlook, @mention it in Word, Excel, and PowerPoint comments, hand it a task by email, and keep working while it continues. The agent can also work with other agents, tools, apps, and services using its own approved credentials and scoped permissions, instead of borrowing the credentials of the person who built it.

That last point is the real change. Microsoft's new account model lets agents use their own approved credentials rather than relying on an individual user's or maker's credentials. Using an agent's own credentials improves the ability to distinguish its actions from a person's actions and reduces dependence on any individual maker's account. An agent with its own account is a participant in its own right. Work can be attributed to the agent, process owners keep control over its scope, and administrators can scope its access and manage its lifecycle. Microsoft says additional access policies, quality, cost management, and lifecycle controls are planned in the coming months. Microsoft announced agent accounts on October 15 as part of capabilities rolling out through Frontier. On November 18, it said dedicated accounts would enter public preview in December 2026.

Memory and triggers make it persistent

An account alone does not make an agent a teammate. Two more pieces do. Business process memory lets the agent carry preferences and process context from one cycle to the next, so what one person explains to it becomes a shared resource for everyone else on the process. Triggers in the Microsoft Copilot harness let an agent start work when an external signal arrives, like an email, or on a set cadence, with no prompt needed. Microsoft also plans agent refinements, which would use an agent's activity to recommend improvements for the maker to evaluate, test, and implement. In Microsoft's hypothetical budget-review example, an agent prepares each Tuesday's review by gathering records, checking forecasts, requesting clarifications and summarizing the results, while the finance team retains decision authority.

The shared-memory caveat, in Microsoft's own words

The memory that makes the agent useful also has a boundary worth reading before any agent gets a mailbox. Microsoft's own FAQ for agent accounts says the agent keeps one memory across everyone it works with, so what one person explains benefits the whole team. That also means anything the agent remembers can surface in conversations with others. Microsoft says the agent is guided not to bring up one person's details with another user, but that guidance is not a technical privacy boundary. The People card notice quoted in the FAQ says: "Any files or chats you share can be summarized and included in the agent's responses to any user, even users without permissions to the files or chats and regardless of sensitivity label." The FAQ states that shared memory has no privacy boundary between users, even in one-to-one chats, that end users cannot opt out in this preview, and that an admin cannot turn shared memory off without turning off agent accounts altogether. Everyone who interacts with the agent can view or delete its saved shared memories, and memories are deleted automatically if no one interacts with the agent for 28 days. The FAQ lists what shared memory excludes: personal details such as ID numbers, birth dates, account or card numbers, personal phone numbers, and home addresses; passwords, credentials, secrets, and tokens; anything someone asks it not to remember; judgments about anyone's performance or conduct; health information; and protected characteristics such as religion, disability, or immigration status. Microsoft says the exclusions also cover information already contained in agent instructions or knowledge sources, and conclusions or generalizations inferred from a single remark. For protected characteristics, Microsoft says the agent keeps only the practical effect when it matters for the work, for example "attends the offsite remotely", not the reason. The exclusions describe what shared memory is designed to leave out; they are not a guarantee that information cannot surface through an agent's response.

What is live now, and what is still a preview

The Nov 18 post sorts the release states plainly. Microsoft describes apps in Copilot Studio and Copilot Cowork as generally available, with rollout during November. Agent sharing among multiple makers and the customer conversation harness are also generally available. Natural-language agent and workflow creation through Cowork and Code is in public preview, as are command-line tools and Copilot Studio MCP. Dedicated agent accounts reach public preview in December 2026. The November 18 announcement does not specify pricing or licensing requirements for the December agent-account preview.

The evidence Microsoft offers

Microsoft reports two operational examples and one set of internal model-evaluation results. Asurion's finance team increased touchless invoice processing from less than 20 percent to more than 70 percent after adding a Copilot Studio agent to its finance automation process. Microsoft's own support team built a dynamic resolution agent that reached a fully autonomous resolution rate of over 35 percent in August 2026. In internal evaluations of the Microsoft Copilot harness, pass rates rose from 67.2 percent to 78.0 percent for OpenAI models and from 67.0 percent to 89.1 percent for Anthropic models. Those are internal evaluations, not independent tests. Ooredoo Qatar is named as an early customer, with an executive business-case review agent working across Outlook, Teams, and Word to review proposals, apply company policy and precedents, and prepare decision-ready briefs.

A swarm of agents, a fixed budget, and a human who still approves

Replit launched Missions on Nov 17, 2026. You define the outcome and the spending limit. A swarm of agents plans, builds, and tests toward it, and the approval gates you set stay in place as the work evolves.

On Nov 17, 2026, Replit launched Missions, a way to hand a swarm of AI agents a goal and a budget instead of a single prompt. You define the outcome, and Replit coordinates agents to plan, build, test, and iterate toward it within the budget and boundaries you set. The examples in the announcement are the kind of work teams keep postponing: winning more enterprise customers, improving product performance, or replacing legacy software.

The agreement comes before the work

Before any agent starts, the mission agreement sets two things: the spending limit, and which actions require your permission. You also agree on acceptable tradeoffs, such as offering paid pilots or discounts on annual contracts. Replit gives an example: cap the mission's spending at $1,000 and require approval before the agent contacts prospects, shares a proposal, changes customer-facing sales materials, or makes product commitments. The announcement states that these limits and approval requirements remain in place as the approach evolves. The agents do not get to renegotiate the boundaries because they found a different tactic.

An independent judge, and a hard stop

During setup, Missions asks questions to define success. For a goal like winning the first 10 paying enterprise customers, it asks what counts as an enterprise customer, and whether paid pilots count or success means signed annual contracts above a minimum value. An independent AI judge checks results against those agreed criteria, and the agents use its feedback to refine their work. A mission is marked complete when the criteria are met. If the budget runs out first, work stops with the outcome still unresolved. That stop is the control that matters most. A swarm that can keep spending until it succeeds is a runaway cost. A swarm that stops when the money runs out is a bounded experiment.

The self-test: DataCat in two days

Replit used itself as the first example. The team wanted application monitoring in-house, so it gave Missions the goal of building DataCat, a platform it could customize and run on its own infrastructure. In two days, within a $3,000 budget, a swarm of agents built a working platform with dashboards, alerts, and application tracing, then tested it against measurable performance and reliability goals. To test whether DataCat could help diagnose a failure, the team deliberately introduced a database bug. DataCat raised an alert in 38 seconds, against a 60-second target, and traced the failure to the exact database query and its logs. In a three-hour load test, DataCat stored 26 million records of application activity and 4.4 million log entries with no data lost, while searches and alerts kept running. All 36 data-correctness checks and 15 alert-reliability checks passed. Replit is open-sourcing a self-hostable version of DataCat.

The swarm numbers, and their limits

Replit also ran a benchmark across 13 representative tasks using NumPy. With Auto (Max), a single agent achieved speedups of up to 4 times. A 16-agent swarm reached 17 times, and a 32-agent swarm reached 19 times. Auto (Power) showed the same pattern at 2, 7, and 9 times. On E-Commerce Bench, where an agent starts with 100,000 yen and runs online stores for a simulated year, Missions grew that to 4.82 million yen in a single run, roughly 48 times the starting money, which Replit calls a state-of-the-art result more than three times the best on the public leaderboard.

Read the caveats Replit wrote into the announcement itself. The controlled tests do not establish performance against the original platform's hosted service or on Replit's production systems. The build comparison used one run per setup on identical cloud machines, scored only shared product areas, and used the original platform's agent version 7.56.2 to send data. API compatibility was a separate test suite from data correctness and alert reliability. These are company-reported figures from company-run tests, not independent benchmarks.

What to watch

Whether the budget stop holds in customer use, and whether the independent judge's checks match what a human would accept. Whether parallel agents duplicate work or make inconsistent decisions on the same goal. And whether the fixed budget makes teams willing to try work they kept postponing. No price is published in the announcement. Replit directs inquiries to its sales team.

Claude linked billing: the key never changes hands.

Anthropic put linked billing into beta on Nov 17, 2026. A customer links their Claude organization to an app once, sets a spend limit, and pays without sharing an API key. The requests still run in the platform's organization.

On Nov 17, 2026, Anthropic put linked billing into beta on the Claude Platform. The change is small and it matters. A customer can now pay for Claude API usage inside a third-party app without handing that app an API key.

The old pattern was the problem. Paying for Claude inside a coding agent or workflow tool usually meant sharing an API key. Anthropic's own article says the key works like a password: anyone who has it can make requests billed to the customer, so the platform has to keep it safe. That meant customer keys stored on someone else's servers, encrypted and rotated by someone else, with the spend landing on the customer's bill either way.

Linked billing replaces the key with a link. The customer connects their Claude Platform organization to the app once, through an approval page Anthropic hosts. The app then chooses on each request whether the platform or the customer pays, by setting a billing_policy field. The customer approves each linked platform, sets a monthly spend limit for it, and can unlink it at any time from the Claude Console. If a request marked for the customer cannot be billed to them, the API refuses it instead of quietly charging the platform. The platform can see each linked customer's request count, token usage, and models used in Console, or through the Usage and Cost Analytics API in beta.

The customer keeps their existing billing relationship with Anthropic. They can draw down from an existing commitment or pay as they go. Anthropic lists 19 platforms supporting linked billing at launch: Asana, ClickHouse, Factory, Gumloop, Harness, Higgsfield AI, Kilo Code, Linear, Major, Mintlify, Omni, OpenArt, Niteshift, Railway, Ramp, Runlayer, Tessera, Tines, and Warp.

The part to read twice is where the requests run. They run in the platform's organization, not the customer's. Anthropic's article states it plainly: settings in the customer's organization, such as IP allowlists, encryption keys, and data retention, do not apply. The documentation goes further. The customer's own data arrangements do not apply to these requests, including zero data retention, a business associate agreement, and customer-managed encryption keys. The docs say not to link customers who need HIPAA, FERPA, or CMEK coverage for this usage. Only the billing moves to the customer. The execution, the rate limits, the model access, the service tier, and the data handling stay with the platform.

Eligibility is narrow for now. A customer can be billed this way only if their Claude Platform organization pays Anthropic directly, by prepaid credits or invoiced billing. Organizations billed through a cloud marketplace, AWS, Google Cloud, or Azure, cannot be billed through the integration, and neither can organizations that hold only a Claude subscription plan. Requests for those organizations are refused with a 403. The monthly API credits that come with Claude Max, Ultra, and Team plans cannot pay for these requests either. Anthropic also excludes credits from partner, venture-capital, and startup programs. The supported endpoints are /v1/messages and /v1/chat/completions, although customer-billed /v1/messages requests cannot use billable code-execution features and return a 400 error if they do. Message Batches does not support billing the customer yet. Anthropic warns the beta can change: endpoints, parameters, and behavior can change during the beta, and it might not be suitable for production use.

Five checks worth keeping before linking an organization to any platform

  1. Confirm who pays on each request. The platform decides per request. Ask which requests the customer pays for and which the platform covers, and get the answer in writing before the first link.
  2. Set the spend limit before the first request, not after the first bill. The customer sets a monthly limit per platform in Console. A limit set late is a bill already sent.
  3. Read the execution split, not just the billing split. The customer's IP allowlists, encryption keys, and retention terms do not travel with the request. If any of those is a requirement, the customer should not link.
  4. Check eligibility before promising it. Marketplace-billed organizations and plan-only organizations are refused. Confirm the organization pays Anthropic directly before telling a customer this is available.
  5. Know what the unlink does and does not do. Unlinking stops future billing. It does not pull back requests already made or data already handled under the platform's terms.

What to watch

whether Anthropic extends linked billing to Message Batches and Managed Agents, whether the HIPAA, FERPA, and CMEK exclusion lifts, and whether other model providers copy the pattern, so a customer can pay inside any tool without ever handing over a key.

Two agents negotiated a Treasury trade. Humans still approved it.

On Nov 17, 2026, BNY announced that it and Microsoft had completed a pilot in which AI agents from the two firms negotiated a US Treasury bill trade under defined controls, while human traders reviewed and approved the proposed terms before execution.

BNY announced on Nov 17, 2026 what it calls an industry-first pilot trade negotiation of a US Treasury bill using AI agents. The agents exchanged offers and counteroffers and settled on terms inside preset limits. They did not execute anything. Human traders at both firms reviewed and approved the proposed terms before the trade was completed, and the booking went through the firms' existing systems. The negotiation was the news. The approval was the point.

The control layer is the announcement

At the center of the trade was BNY's Agentic Negotiation Protocol, or ANP, a patent-pending control layer for authorized agents from different organizations to negotiate with each other. ANP verifies the participants and lets them connect securely, then lets the agents exchange offers and counteroffers and confirms both sides share the same understanding of the proposed terms. Participant-side controls set predefined rules and AI safety checks, keeping the agents within tightly defined parameters based on approved pricing inputs. Before trader approval is requested, ANP's ledger checks that both parties' agents submitted matching terms. The process preserves a record of who participated, the messages exchanged, the control checks, and the human decisions, so the negotiation can be reviewed and reconstructed later.

Three limits are stated plainly in the release. Agents cannot execute the trade on behalf of either firm. Human traders retain authority over final decisions and actions. Trades are booked through existing internal trading systems, not through the agent layer. The agents negotiated. People decided. Existing systems recorded it.

How the trade happened

Microsoft Treasury gave its agent specific trading instructions, and the agent connected to BNY through a secure agent gateway to ANP. BNY controls validated the counterparty, the security, the size, the settlement terms, and the available pricing. Once those checks passed, the agents negotiated prices within their authorized limits. When they reached agreement, the proposed terms were independently submitted for comparison against ANP's central ledger to confirm both parties had the same understanding of the transaction. BNY and Microsoft traders then reviewed and approved the proposed terms, with execution and booking completed through the firms' existing systems and controls. Bloomberg reported the same day that the agents haggled and agreed on a price before humans approved the transaction.

What the release does not say

The release states no trade size, no maturity, no yield, and no price. It does not say how long the negotiation took, or how many offers and counteroffers passed between the agents. The announcement describes one pilot trade negotiation between BNY and Microsoft, and "industry-first" is BNY's characterization, not an independently established global first. The release also does not claim the agents trade better than humans. It claims the process is more consistent than the traditional message-based negotiation, and that it frees traders to focus on higher-value work while keeping human accountability and existing risk and transaction-processing controls in place. Those are claims about the process, not about the price.

Where it goes next

BNY plans to continue testing ANP, with a focus on expanding beyond the initial bilateral use case and evaluating additional negotiated workflows. Over time, future applications could include repo and other contractual negotiations. The pattern to watch is governed autonomy, not full automation: authorized agents, defined limits, a matching-terms check, human approval, and a complete record. That is the same shape as every approval-gated system in this series, now tested on a Treasury trade at a bank that oversees $63.4 trillion in assets under custody and administration and $2.2 trillion in assets under management, as of Sep 30, 2026.

What to watch

Whether other banks and dealers join the protocol, and whether it holds up outside a bilateral pilot with a willing counterparty. Whether the ledger check catches a mismatch in a live test, and what happens when it does. Whether regulators ask to see the record ANP preserves, and whether that record satisfies them. And whether the next test, repo or a contractual negotiation, keeps the same rule: the agents can bargain, but only people can commit.

Fed governor warns of AI agents attempting bank-account actions. Four guardrails, no new rule.

Federal Reserve Governor Michael Barr said AI agents have already tried to take actions on bank accounts. The speech sets four expectations, and no new rule.

Federal Reserve Vice Chair for Supervision Michael S. Barr gave a speech on Nov 13, 2026, titled "AI Agents and the Future of Banking," at the Financial Services Roundtable in Washington, D.C. The speech opens with a warning: AI agents "have already been observed trying to take actions on behalf of bank accounts, including attempts to log in to bank accounts and initiate wire transfers." The speech is the Federal Reserve's first public position on agentic AI in banking, and it sets expectations rather than new rules.

Barr laid out four expectations for banks that use AI agents. First, the bank must be able to explain, in plain terms, what the agent did and why, even when the system uses a chain of models, because "a model that cannot be understood cannot be governed." Second, the bank must be able to show the agent acted only within its delegated authority, and that the authority was granted by the right person, for the right scope, for the right duration. Third, the bank must be able to prove the agent's actions were recorded and auditable, with a trail that survives the agent itself. Fourth, the bank must be able to stop the agent, in real time, when it acts outside its bounds.

The speech also named the risks plainly. An agent that can move money can move the wrong amount, to the wrong party, at a speed no human review can match. An agent that can read a customer's records can expose them. An agent that can act on behalf of a bank can bind the bank to a commitment the bank never intended. Barr said banks should not deploy agents into production until they can meet the four expectations, and that supervisors will ask for the evidence, not the marketing.

The pattern is familiar from our own work. A check that runs before the consequential move, a named person who approves, and a record that travels with the work. The Federal Reserve is now asking banks for the same pattern, in the same terms, for the same reason: an agent that moves money without a check is a liability, not a convenience.

What to watch

Whether the four expectations become examination questions, whether banks publish their agent governance, and whether the same pattern reaches other regulated industries.

Finance agents that show their work before anything is committed

Chargebee launched Arc on Nov 18, 2026. Three finance agents handle contract ingestion, receivables, and revenue recognition, and every action is shown, cited, and held for review before anything is committed.

On Nov 18, 2026, Chargebee announced Arc, a new agentic platform that runs complete finance workflows as one connected, traceable thread, from contract to invoice to revenue. At launch, Arc's pre-built agents solve three finance jobs: contract ingestion, receivables, and revenue recognition. A workspace called Agent Studio lets teams build custom agents on the same foundations. Arc is open for early access now.

Three agents, one thread

The contract ingestion agent reads a signed contract and brings a proposed billing setup and open questions to the finance team, with every extracted field linked back to its source, including the customer, items, prices, entitlements, price ramps, billing dates, and payment terms. The receivables agent builds a context graph of each customer's contracts, usage, and payment history, then prepares collections work, such as drafting the reply to a promise to pay or a question about a usage charge. Human teams approve, edit, or reject each proposal, and the agent works within each company's own rules and escalation thresholds. The revenue recognition agent breaks each contract into performance obligations, applies company policy, including prepaid credits, overages, and rollovers, and reallocates price when a contract changes, so teams can trace how each amendment changed revenue.

The governance layer is the product

Arc runs on four building blocks: tasks, drafts, comments, and citations. Tasks break complex work into steps, coordinate it across tools, teams, and time, and keep a clear record of each step and who took it. Drafts give teams a safe place to check proposed work, so nothing irreversible happens until someone signs off. Comments let agents flag ambiguity and collaborate with users in context. Citations show where each value came from, so review and verification go faster. Every action is shown, cited, and held for review before anything is committed.

Arc carries each piece of finance work from start to finish as a single thread, logging every step with its author and its rationale, so the audit trail is complete whether a human or an agent did the work. When a human corrects an agent's treatment, a built-in learning loop remembers that correction the next time. Arc also works beyond Chargebee, pulling context from and acting across systems such as contract management, CRM, and email.

Why finance first

Finance teams rely on different systems of record for auditable, defensible numbers, but those systems do not capture the day-to-day operational context scattered across Slack threads, customer contracts, collections emails, and employees' heads. Chargebee says hundreds of its customers have already built agents, dashboards, and automations with its MCP release, and Arc grew from what those teams built. The quote from co-founder and CEO Krish Subramanian in the release puts it plainly: finance teams need systems that provide a shared foundation for humans and agents, and a standard of consistency and accuracy for every unit of work.
