A swarm of agents, a fixed budget, and a human who still approves

Replit launched Missions on Nov 17, 2026. You define the outcome and the spending limit. A swarm of agents plans, builds, and tests toward it, and the approval gates you set stay in place as the work evolves.

On Nov 17, 2026, Replit launched Missions, a way to hand a swarm of AI agents a goal and a budget instead of a single prompt. You define the outcome, and Replit coordinates agents to plan, build, test, and iterate toward it within the budget and boundaries you set. The examples in the announcement are the kind of work teams keep postponing: winning more enterprise customers, improving product performance, or replacing legacy software.

The agreement comes before the work

Before any agent starts, the mission agreement sets two things: the spending limit, and which actions require your permission. You also agree on acceptable tradeoffs, such as offering paid pilots or discounts on annual contracts. Replit gives an example: cap the mission's spending at $1,000 and require approval before the agent contacts prospects, shares a proposal, changes customer-facing sales materials, or makes product commitments. The announcement states that these limits and approval requirements remain in place as the approach evolves. The agents do not get to renegotiate the boundaries because they found a different tactic.

An independent judge, and a hard stop

During setup, Missions asks questions to define success. For a goal like winning the first 10 paying enterprise customers, it asks what counts as an enterprise customer, and whether paid pilots count or success means signed annual contracts above a minimum value. An independent AI judge checks results against those agreed criteria, and the agents use its feedback to refine their work. A mission is marked complete when the criteria are met. If the budget runs out first, work stops with the outcome still unresolved. That stop is the control that matters most. A swarm that can keep spending until it succeeds is a runaway cost. A swarm that stops when the money runs out is a bounded experiment.

The self-test: DataCat in two days

Replit used itself as the first example. The team wanted application monitoring in-house, so it gave Missions the goal of building DataCat, a platform it could customize and run on its own infrastructure. In two days, within a $3,000 budget, a swarm of agents built a working platform with dashboards, alerts, and application tracing, then tested it against measurable performance and reliability goals. To test whether DataCat could help diagnose a failure, the team deliberately introduced a database bug. DataCat raised an alert in 38 seconds, against a 60-second target, and traced the failure to the exact database query and its logs. In a three-hour load test, DataCat stored 26 million records of application activity and 4.4 million log entries with no data lost, while searches and alerts kept running. All 36 data-correctness checks and 15 alert-reliability checks passed. Replit is open-sourcing a self-hostable version of DataCat.

The swarm numbers, and their limits

Replit also ran a benchmark across 13 representative tasks using NumPy. With Auto (Max), a single agent achieved speedups of up to 4 times. A 16-agent swarm reached 17 times, and a 32-agent swarm reached 19 times. Auto (Power) showed the same pattern at 2, 7, and 9 times. On E-Commerce Bench, where an agent starts with 100,000 yen and runs online stores for a simulated year, Missions grew that to 4.82 million yen in a single run, roughly 48 times the starting money, which Replit calls a state-of-the-art result more than three times the best on the public leaderboard.

Read the caveats Replit wrote into the announcement itself. The controlled tests do not establish performance against the original platform's hosted service or on Replit's production systems. The build comparison used one run per setup on identical cloud machines, scored only shared product areas, and used the original platform's agent version 7.56.2 to send data. API compatibility was a separate test suite from data correctness and alert reliability. These are company-reported figures from company-run tests, not independent benchmarks.

What to watch

Whether the budget stop holds in customer use, and whether the independent judge's checks match what a human would accept. Whether parallel agents duplicate work or make inconsistent decisions on the same goal. And whether the fixed budget makes teams willing to try work they kept postponing. No price is published in the announcement. Replit directs inquiries to its sales team.
