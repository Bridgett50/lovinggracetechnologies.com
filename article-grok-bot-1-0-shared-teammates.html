The AI teammate joined the meeting. Who approves what it learns?

xAI launched Grok Bot 1.0 on Nov 2, 2026. Team Bots now work in Slack, Microsoft Teams, and video calls, and they offer to take on work before anyone asks. The announcement does not say who approves what a shared Bot learns, or whose accounts it acts through. Those are the questions to ask before sharing one.

xAI says Grok Bot 1.0 is now available. Its announcement describes the product as more collaborative, proactive, and capable of completing work across team conversations, connected applications, and browser-based tasks. On Nov 2, 2026, xAI published the launch post for Grok Bot 1.0, and the post says it is available starting that day. 1.0's three main changes are that Bots can be shared with a team and brought into Slack, Microsoft Teams, and video calls; that Bots can proactively offer to take on work; and that Bots are faster and more reliable at completing complex, browser-based work, including writing documents and building presentations.

The shared part is the change that matters. A Team Bot can be shared with specific people or groups, and worked with in the app, in Slack, or in Microsoft Teams. In conversations it can follow discussions involving several people, understand who is involved, and contribute information or offer to pick up work. During a product launch, xAI's example has a Bot notice that new customer research points to a different message than the team is considering, bring that into the discussion, then coordinate with another Bot to update campaign materials once the team agrees on a direction. Bots can also join video calls and participate through the meeting chat, bringing information from earlier conversations into the discussion and keeping track of decisions. Team Bots are available on Team and Enterprise plans. Meetings are available on Team, Enterprise, and Ultra plans, and enterprise admins can turn meetings on in settings.

The second change is initiative. A Bot can monitor ongoing projects, recognize when something needs attention, and suggest next steps based on what it knows about the team's goals, processes, and priorities. It can follow up on unfinished work and bring people in when a decision requires their judgment. xAI's example is a launch Bot that notices an upcoming deadline and prepares the materials the team needs. xAI says Bots respect individual permissions, ask before sensitive actions, and check whether information is appropriate for public channels, and that Bots can now better distinguish actions that are already authorized from those requiring another request for permission.

The third change is memory, and it is the one that needs the closest look. As Bots work with different people, xAI says they learn more about the team, its goals and its work. The announcement also says a Bot can suggest changes to how it works based on what it has learned. The announcement also lists new Bot settings and analytics, which let teams manage what a Team Bot knows and see how the team uses it, plus interactive Pages that Bots can create and users can edit together, better sign-in handling, improved connections to Gmail, Google Calendar, and GitHub, and fewer resources needed to complete tasks.

What the announcement does not say is who approves what a Bot learns before the whole team inherits it, or whose connected accounts a shared Bot acts through. Those are the two questions the launch leaves open, and they are the ones that matter most for a team deciding whether to share a Bot. A personal assistant acts for one person. A team Bot acts for many people, in shared channels, and carries what it learned from one person into work for another. The safeguards xAI names are permissions, asking before sensitive actions, and checking what is appropriate to share in a public channel. The record that proves those safeguards worked is the approval log: what the Bot was taught, who approved it, and what it did. The announcement does not describe that log.

Five checks worth keeping before a shared Bot joins the team

  1. Share with specific people first. A Team Bot is shared with specific people or groups, not the whole company at once. Start narrow, and widen only when the team is comfortable.
  2. Ask who approves what the Bot learns. The announcement does not name an approval step for shared learning. Before sharing a Bot, ask how a lesson one person teaches reaches everyone else, and who can stop it.
  3. Ask whose accounts it acts through. The announcement does not say whose connected apps a shared Bot uses. Do not assume it is yours, or that it is everyone's. Get the answer before the Bot touches a shared system.
  4. Keep meeting participation visible, and check what it carries into public channels. A Bot that joins a call and takes part in the chat should be named in the invite, and the record should show what it brought into the discussion. xAI says Bots check whether information is appropriate to share in a public channel. Hold them to it.
  5. Treat proactive work as a proposal. A Bot that spots unfinished work and offers to take it on should show the work first. The team decides, then the Bot acts.

What to watch

Whether xAI publishes the learning-approval and credential answers, whether meeting participation grows beyond the chat, and whether other agent products publish their own shared-learning controls. The news this week is that the teammate is no longer personal. It belongs to the team now. The announcement does not yet say who approves what it learns, or whose accounts it acts through. Until it does, those are questions to ask before sharing a Bot.
