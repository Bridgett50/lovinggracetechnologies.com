Self-hosted AI vs hosted AI: what changes when you own the model

Self-hosted and hosted AI differ in where your files live, who controls the infrastructure, and what happens when the engagement ends.

The question is not which model is smarter. The question is where your files live, who controls the infrastructure, and what happens to your data and system if the engagement ends. That is what changes between self-hosted and hosted AI.

Hosted AI runs on someone else's service. You send your text to their system, and their terms decide what happens to it. It is fast to start and easy to use, and it fits work where the records are not sensitive and the controls are simple.

Self-hosted model serving means operating the model and its inference stack rather than relying entirely on a hosted model API. The infrastructure provider, data-handling arrangements and administrative controls must still be identified separately. It takes more setup, and it fits work where control matters: records that must not leave your system, checks that must run before anything moves, and an exit that must be clean.

Here is how we run both, in our own work:

Our Glimmer model is Muse-Glimmer-30B, an open-weight model served through vLLM on Modal using one A100-80GB GPU. The deployment uses scale-to-zero operation and temperature 0.0 for redaction. Our Spark model is muse-spark-1.3 via the Meta API, used for cited briefs only, on redacted text only. Our default Offline mode calls no model at all.

Glimmer provides the self-hosted model-serving path. Spark is accessed through the Meta API and is restricted to cited briefs using redacted text. Default Offline mode makes no model calls. Either way, we say plainly where your files live, who controls the infrastructure, and what happens to your data and system if the engagement ends.

Self-hosted model serving does not mean the company owns the underlying model weights, GPU provider or physical infrastructure.
