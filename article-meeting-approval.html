Who approves what the AI drafts?

A meeting assistant that can pay on its own is a risk, not a helper. The fix is a separate checker and a person's tap.

Meetings end in commitments. An AI that turns meeting talk into payments must be checked against what was actually agreed, not trusted on its own reading of the conversation. If the same system drafts and approves, a misread becomes a payment.

Draft, check, approve

Second Seat works in three steps. First, AI drafts each payment from the meeting. Second, a separate checker compares the draft against the owner's own words and blocks anything that does not match, like a changed bank account or an inflated amount. Third, nothing is paid until the owner approves it with one tap. Every payment keeps the quote it came from as its receipt.

What the demo proved

In a sandbox demonstration using PayPal's test environment, a fake bank-change request was blocked, and a clean $480 payment was approved and captured as completed. That was a demonstration, not a client payment, and it is described here as one. The point stands: the checker caught what the drafter missed, and the owner's tap was the step that released the money.

The principle travels

The same separation applies to any agent that acts on your behalf. Drafting and checking are different jobs, and approval belongs to a person. That is the standard we build to, in Second Seat and in every agent we build for clients.
