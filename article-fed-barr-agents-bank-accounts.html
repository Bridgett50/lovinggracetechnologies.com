Fed governor warns of AI agents attempting bank-account actions. Four guardrails, no new rule.

Federal Reserve Governor Michael Barr said AI agents have already tried to take actions on bank accounts. The speech sets four expectations, and no new rule.

Federal Reserve Vice Chair for Supervision Michael S. Barr gave a speech on Nov 13, 2026, titled "AI Agents and the Future of Banking," at the Financial Services Roundtable in Washington, D.C. The speech opens with a warning: AI agents "have already been observed trying to take actions on behalf of bank accounts, including attempts to log in to bank accounts and initiate wire transfers." The speech is the Federal Reserve's first public position on agentic AI in banking, and it sets expectations rather than new rules.

Barr laid out four expectations for banks that use AI agents. First, the bank must be able to explain, in plain terms, what the agent did and why, even when the system uses a chain of models, because "a model that cannot be understood cannot be governed." Second, the bank must be able to show the agent acted only within its delegated authority, and that the authority was granted by the right person, for the right scope, for the right duration. Third, the bank must be able to prove the agent's actions were recorded and auditable, with a trail that survives the agent itself. Fourth, the bank must be able to stop the agent, in real time, when it acts outside its bounds.

The speech also named the risks plainly. An agent that can move money can move the wrong amount, to the wrong party, at a speed no human review can match. An agent that can read a customer's records can expose them. An agent that can act on behalf of a bank can bind the bank to a commitment the bank never intended. Barr said banks should not deploy agents into production until they can meet the four expectations, and that supervisors will ask for the evidence, not the marketing.

The pattern is familiar from our own work. A check that runs before the consequential move, a named person who approves, and a record that travels with the work. The Federal Reserve is now asking banks for the same pattern, in the same terms, for the same reason: an agent that moves money without a check is a liability, not a convenience.

What to watch

Whether the four expectations become examination questions, whether banks publish their agent governance, and whether the same pattern reaches other regulated industries.
